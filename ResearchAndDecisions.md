# Client, competitor and accessibility research

**Project:** Between the Lines — Create That UX (Cardan)

## Design question

How might an interactive website make unnecessary reading and navigation barriers tangible to people unfamiliar with accessibility, then help them identify specific improvements—without claiming to reproduce dyslexia?

## 1. Client understanding: Cardan

Cardan is a digital-accessibility specialist based in Tilburg, Netherlands. Its published services include accessibility testing, training and awareness-building. Cardan's own [Ervaringsplein](https://www.cardan.com/diensten/bewustwording/ervaringsplein) presents five interactive activities to demonstrate digital barriers and explain how they can be addressed. The stated outcomes include understanding what accessibility is, whom it affects, common barriers, and how to fix them.

Cardan lists the values *accessible, helpful, reliable, innovative and passionate* on its [About page](https://www.cardan.com/over-cardan). These principles suggest that a successful student concept should be educational and usable, not simply difficult for its own sake.

**Design response:** Give visitors a controlled hands-on challenge, explain the barrier, then let them try a clearer alternative. Keep the real website navigable and the intentionally difficult activities optional.

## 2. Relevant experiences and comparison

### A. Cardan — Ervaringsplein (client reference)
- **Observed from public material:** Interactive experiences and specialist guidance connect the *experience* of a barrier to practical accessibility awareness.
- **Transferable lesson:** Pair friction with actionable explanation; do not stop at emotional reaction.
- **Our distinction:** A focused self-guided web experience about reading effort, designed for online delivery.

### B. Understood.org — Through My Eyes (comparable educational experience)
- **Observed from public material:** Understood pairs lived-experience stories, interactive simulations, and expert educational resources about ADHD, dyslexia and dyscalculia.
- **Transferable lesson:** Personal context and an action-oriented debrief are important, especially because disability simulations can be misunderstood.
- **Our distinction:** Rather than claim to tell a person's story, Between the Lines demonstrates avoidable web-interface barriers and directs viewers to authentic dyslexic voices. We do **not** invent first-person testimony.

### C. W3C — Before and After Demonstration (BAD)
- **Observed from official guidance:** W3C provides an intentionally inaccessible site and an accessible version of the same site, with explanations of the barriers and repairs.
- **Transferable lesson:** Keep the information constant and let visitors directly compare the accessibility impact of different design choices.
- **Our distinction:** Connect the before/after demonstration to three interactive reading tasks and individual reflection.

### D. Funkify — Disability Simulator browser extension
- **Observed from the provider's public material:** Funkify offers simulations across vision, cognitive, reading and motor categories, plus analysis tools.
- **Transferable lesson:** Trying tasks directly can start a conversation about barriers and design responsibilities.
- **Important caution:** Some simulations visually distort or move letters. Between the Lines does **not** present such effects as a literal description of dyslexia; it uses clearly-labelled invented tasks and redirects to genuine perspectives.
- **Our distinction:** Self-contained educational flow with explicit design improvements, no browser extension or account required.

### E. W3C cognitive accessibility patterns (design benchmark)
- **Observed from official guidance:** Users may need short and clear content, predictable navigation, sufficient time and flexible presentation; clear structure supports finding information.
- **Transferable lesson:** Show the same information before and after redesign, including adjustable time and reading options.

*Methodological limitation:* This is a desk review of publicly documented approaches, **not** a hands-on comparative usability experiment or confirmation that their outcomes generalize to this product.

## 3. Dyslexia accuracy

The International Dyslexia Association's 2025 definition describes difficulties with word reading and/or spelling involving speed, accuracy, or both, with varied expression and complex causes. Dyslexia is **not** defined as literally seeing bouncing, reversed or scrambled letters.

**Design response:** Label the decoding activity as an analogy; distinguish invented barriers from dyslexia itself. Do not claim to diagnose, reproduce a disability or prove increased empathy.

## 4. User-feedback-driven changes

- **Previous interaction:** A basic one-question portal-finding task was evaluated informally by the project creator, who found it too easy.
- **Recorded teacher feedback:** Kadian said the prototype looked good but the challenges were too simple. She recommended stronger challenges, competitor exploration and testing with dyslexic and nondyslexic participants (FeedPulse Checkpoint 7, 3 November 2026; student-recorded summary).
- **Current revision:** Three different scenarios, each with two related facts to extract; a retryable answer-checking step; short optional hints; purposeful debriefing; direct interactive comparison; accessible exit and time controls.
- **Still needed for validation:** Real participant sessions, ideally including someone with dyslexia, and feedback on realism, respectfulness, difficulty and practical learning. Do not infer success without that evidence.
